Specialist technical supervision

Technical Supervision for Villa Builds in Koh Phangan

Technical supervision is engineering-level review of a villa build on Koh Phangan, focused on the elements that are expensive to fix later: reinforcement, concrete, waterproofing, drainage and building services. FORMA checks these at defined hold points, before they are covered up by the next stage of work.

Beyond finishes: the systems that make a building work

Some quality issues are visible on a walk-through; others are buried in a wall or a roof void and only reveal themselves years later as a leak, a crack or a failed system. Technical supervision goes beyond general site inspection to focus specifically on structural execution, building services installation and the technical junctions most prone to failure in a tropical marine environment, coordinating directly with the structural and MEP engineers where their input is required.

Scope of work

Structural verification

Checking reinforcement, formwork, concrete quality and connection details against the structural engineer's drawings.

MEP compliance

Verifying electrical, plumbing, drainage and air-conditioning installations against the engineered design and applicable standards.

Building envelope detailing

Waterproofing, junction and shading detail checks at the points most exposed to tropical weathering.

Consultant coordination

Managing site queries between contractor and specialist engineers to resolve technical issues quickly.

Where technical failures actually originate

Most long-term technical failures in tropical construction trace back to a small set of causes: inadequate concrete cover leading to reinforcement corrosion, poorly detailed roof and terrace waterproofing, and MEP penetrations through the building envelope that are not properly sealed. Technical supervision targets exactly these points, at the stage when they are still correctable.

What technical supervision means for a villa build

Technical supervision is engineering-focused review of the parts of a building that cannot be judged by eye once finished. It verifies that structure, drainage, waterproofing and building services are executed as designed by the responsible engineers, and it does so at hold points, meaning moments when work must be inspected before the next stage is allowed to cover it.

It is narrower and deeper than general site supervision, and it is not project management. It does not replace the structural or MEP engineer, and it does not redesign anything. Where a design question arises on site, the query goes to the engineer who owns that design.

Hold points that matter most

A hold point is useful only if it falls before the work disappears. For a typical concrete-framed villa these are the moments when a mistake is cheapest to correct and most expensive to discover later.

  • Foundation and excavation base, before concrete is placed
  • Reinforcement and formwork before each structural pour
  • Pipe, conduit and sleeve positions before slabs and walls are closed
  • Waterproofing layers before screeds, tiles or soil cover them
  • Services rough-in before ceilings and wall finishes
  • Testing of water, drainage and electrical systems before handover

Structure: what is checked before concrete is placed

Before a pour the supervisor compares bar size, spacing, laps, anchorage and cover with the structural drawings, and checks that formwork is stable, clean and correctly positioned. Cover, the distance between steel and the concrete surface, matters especially in a humid, salt-laden climate because it determines how long the steel stays protected.

On a slope, attention extends to retaining walls, drainage behind them and the connection between footings and the ground. If conditions found on excavation differ from those assumed in the design, the engineer is informed and a decision is recorded before work continues.

Building services and the tropical envelope

Electrical, plumbing and air-conditioning work is checked against the engineered design, including routes, falls on drainage pipes, condensate disposal, earthing and the protection of cables from moisture. Penetrations through roofs and walls are a frequent source of leaks, so sealing and flashing at each one is looked at before cladding or finishes hide it.

Waterproofing at roofs, terraces, bathrooms and planters is inspected for continuity, upturns at edges and the detailing around drains and pipes. Heat and ultraviolet exposure affect exposed membranes differently from buried ones, and the specification should say which is which.

Technical supervision, construction supervision and project management compared

Technical supervision answers whether the hidden, engineered work is correct. Construction supervision answers whether the visible work matches the drawings and is well made. Project management answers whether cost, programme and coordination are under control. Each can stand alone, and each has a different reader and a different record.

For an owner building with a separate contractor, technical supervision is most valuable where the contractor's own site team has limited engineering depth, or where the structure is unusual, for example cantilevers, long spans, basements or steep-site foundations.

Records the owner keeps

Each hold-point inspection produces a dated record listing what was checked, what was found and whether the next stage was released. Photographs of reinforcement, pipework and waterproofing are taken before concealment and filed by location.

At the end the owner receives a technical handover file with the inspection records, test results, equipment information and as-built information gathered during the build. This is what a future buyer, lender, insurer or renovation designer will want to see, and it is difficult to reconstruct afterwards.

Timing, access and practical limits

Technical visits must be timed to the contractor's sequence, which changes with weather and deliveries. The contractor needs to give notice before a pour or before closing a wall, and the owner should make that notice a contract requirement. Without it, concealment happens before inspection.

Technical supervision can identify and document a problem; it cannot make a wrong construction right without cost. Appointing it before the structure begins is therefore far more valuable than appointing it after the frame is complete.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is technical supervision different from general construction supervision?

Technical supervision focuses specifically on structural and building-systems compliance with engineered design; general construction supervision covers broader quality, workmanship and progress monitoring. Many projects use both together.

Do you supervise structural work poured by another contractor?

Yes, structural stage inspections for reinforcement and concrete quality are a core part of this service, regardless of who holds the construction contract.

Can you coordinate directly with our structural engineer?

Yes, direct coordination with the project's structural and MEP engineers is central to resolving technical site queries efficiently.

Do you check air conditioning and mechanical systems specifically?

Yes, MEP compliance checks include air conditioning sizing and installation as part of the broader mechanical and electrical review.

What happens if reinforcement or concrete work does not meet specification?

Non-compliant work is documented and flagged before the next stage proceeds, with remediation confirmed by re-inspection rather than accepted on assurance alone.

Is waterproofing inspection included in technical supervision?

Yes, envelope and waterproofing detailing at roofs, terraces and wet areas is one of the specific focus areas of this service.

Do you provide as-built technical drawings at the end of the project?

As-built technical documentation is compiled as part of the technical handover, valuable for future maintenance and any subsequent renovation work.

Can technical supervision be added partway through a build?

Yes, following an initial technical review of work already completed to establish what has and has not been verified to date.
